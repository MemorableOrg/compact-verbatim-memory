\section{Reader rules and planner prompt}
\label{app:rules}

The reader prompt lists the retrieved turns, each as one line with its date and speaker, then the question, then these rules, verbatim:

\begin{quote}\small
Rules: use only the excerpts. Combine and cross-reference every relevant excerpt. If excerpts contradict, prefer the most recent one and mention the change. Keep exact names, numbers and dates. Follow any standing instruction or preference the user gave in the excerpts. For ordering questions list the items in chronological order, one per line. For time questions use the dates shown. For counts, first list every instance with its date, then give the number. Resolve relative times (``two weeks later'', ``the day before'') against the dates shown and state the resulting date. If the user stated a rule or preference more than once, the latest statement wins; ignore rules the user later withdrew. When listing events, merge repeated mentions of the same event. For summaries, cover each phase of the topic in date order. Only when the question itself asks for the current, latest or present value, answer with the most recent value and mention the earlier value as history. For the time between two named events, use the dates given where those events are stated, show both dates and the arithmetic, and note any later change to a date in one short clause. If the excerpts mention the topic but not the specific detail asked for, say that the conversation does not include that detail; never fill in details that are not written in the excerpts.
\end{quote}

The planner prompt:

\begin{quote}\small
You plan searches over a long chat history between a user and an assistant. Given the user's question, reply with JSON only: \{``type'': one of ``lookup'' (one fact), ``aggregate'' (combine facts spread over many sessions, counts, lists, comparisons), ``order'' (sequence or timeline of events), ``summary'' (summarize a topic), ``queries'': 2 to 4 short search queries that together cover every part of the question, using the words the conversation would likely use\}.
\end{quote}

\section{Compaction rules}
\label{app:compaction}

Markdown removed: heading marks (\texttt{\#} at line start), emphasis stars and underscores, horizontal rules, table separator rows and code-fence lines; table pipes become a spaced bar. The filler rule applies to user and assistant turns; the repeat rule to assistant turns only. Filler sentences removed when the whole sentence matches: ``Great/Good/Excellent question/point/idea'', ``Sure/Absolutely/Of course/Certainly'', ``I hope this helps'', ``Feel free to ...'', ``Let me know if ...'', ``Happy to help'', ``Thanks for sharing/asking/the update'', ``Good luck'', ``Is there anything else ...'', ``Would you like me to ...?''. Repeated assistant sentences: an assistant sentence over 40 characters whose lower-cased, punctuation-stripped form has appeared earlier in the same conversation is dropped; the first occurrence is kept. On the first 1M conversation the most frequent removals, across the filler and repeat rules, were ``Certainly!'' (388 times) and ``If you have any further questions or need additional assistance, feel free to ask!'' (127 times).

If the rules empty a turn, its first 200 original characters are kept. The rules have failure cases we know of: the emphasis rule removes every pair of stars, so an expression such as \texttt{x**2} loses its operator, and the filler rule matches by sentence shape, so ``Let me know if deployment fails at 17:00.'' is removed although it carries a time. On the first 1M conversation we inspected the removed sentences by frequency: the frequent ones are pleasantries and repeated closings, and we have not read every removed sentence.

\section{8-bit vectors against float vectors}
\label{app:int8}

We ran the search of Section~\ref{sec:search} with the planner and the standing pool switched off, on the 269 development questions of the 500K tier that have gold turns, with compaction on, once with bge-small vectors stored as float32 and once as 8-bit integers (Equation~\ref{eq:quant}), and measured how often the gold turns reach the read set. With float32, every gold turn is in the top 20 for 46.1\% of questions and in the top 60 for 56.1\%, and the share of gold turns found is 56.2\% at 20 and 68.0\% at 60. With 8-bit vectors the four numbers are 45.7\%, 55.8\%, 55.8\% and 67.7\%. One ability moves, by one question each time: information extraction at top 20 falls from 66.7\% to 63.3\% and at top 60 from 83.3\% to 80.0\%; the other eight abilities are unchanged at both cutoffs. Reader input at 60 turns is 26,750 tokens with float32 and 26,769 with 8-bit vectors. An earlier sweep whose settings we did not record gave 48.3\% against 48.0\% at 20 and 59.1\% against 58.7\% at 60; we report the rerun, whose per-question rows we release.

\section{Mem0 build measurement}
\label{app:mem0build}

We ran mem0ai 2.0.0 with its default extraction prompt, gpt-5-mini as the extraction model through an Azure OpenAI endpoint, a local Qdrant vector store and bge-small-en-v1.5 as the embedder. We fed it the first BEAM-1M conversation in Mem0's own batches of two turns and logged every model call. After 683 batches of 855 the metered cost reached \$6.00 and we stopped the run: 6,854,553 input and 2,143,893 output tokens, 3,788 memory entries of 242 characters on average (0.92~MB of text, 5.8~MB of float vectors), 64~MB on disk including Qdrant's index and the change-log database, 260 minutes of wall time at one worker. The figures in the body scale these by 855/683. Between 2.0.0 and the current 2.2.1 Mem0's extraction prompt is unchanged and its add path gains only warnings, input checks and one bug fix, so the measurement applies to the current release.
